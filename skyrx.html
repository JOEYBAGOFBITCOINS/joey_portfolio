SkyRx

Closer when
it counts.

An emergency-drone dispatch simulator set in Phoenix. It explores how an operator could select an aircraft, approve a mission, handle a disruption, and record a confirmed handoff.

01 / Operator workflow

Every response has a state.

The interface makes approval, progress, disruption, and recovery explicit. A map marker reaching its destination does not automatically prove delivery.

  1. Select a request.

    Choose a synthetic call and review modeled routes from real Phoenix station locations to candidate handoff sites.

  2. Approve the mission.

    Review payload availability, modeled flight time, and battery reserve before a simulated launch.

  3. Handle a disruption.

    Inject a lost link, blocked site, or low-battery condition. Inspect the state and choose a recovery action.

  4. Confirm and review.

    Record a fictional responder handoff, follow the return, and inspect or export the mission's event timeline.

02 / Engineering decisions

Make failure understandable.

State before animation.

A deterministic mission model drives the interface and aircraft marker. Launch eligibility is checked at dispatch time. Lost-link conditions freeze simulated progress and identify the position as last known.

Delivery confirmation and aircraft recovery are separate events. Cancelling a request or recovering a grounded aircraft does not silently erase an already confirmed handoff.

A record that can be inspected.

The event stream captures operator decisions and mission transitions. Replay presents those recorded decisions, while JSON export makes the exercise available for review.

Browser-local storage preserves the exercise. Reloading restores it paused so the operator chooses when to continue. No real aircraft or emergency services are connected.

03 / Geography & evidence

Real places. Clear boundaries.

Public geographic context.

The map uses real Phoenix Fire Station 3, 14, and 21 locations and public park geography. Users can switch between street mapping and historical aerial imagery and zoom into the exercise area.

Historical overdose statistics are labeled by year, geography, and measure. They provide context for the demonstration area, not individual victim locations or a claim about current incidents.

A software simulation.

Every call, aircraft assignment, payload inventory, battery, and flight estimate is synthetic. Candidate park locations have not been approved or surveyed for drone landings.

The route model uses straight lines. It does not evaluate airspace, obstacles, real weather, clinical protocols, or flight authorization. The project is independent of BRINC, Phoenix Fire, and Maricopa County.

04 / Verification & next steps

A foundation for deeper work.

Current implementation.

Focused automated tests cover dispatch rules, mission transitions, fault handling, recovery, coordinate conversion, and saved-state validation. Responsive layouts accommodate phone navigation, touch controls, and stacked operational panels.

Visual browser verification remains pending in the current execution environment. The project is a portfolio prototype, not an operational emergency system.

Next engineering increment.

A mock dispatch API, server-owned mission state, authenticated operator roles, and streamed simulation telemetry could extend the prototype beyond its current browser-local model.

Real-world integration would require a separate engineering and operational program with the relevant operators and authorities.
